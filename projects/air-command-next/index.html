GAME DESIGN

One card. Different opportunities.

Four squadrons race aircraft around a board and compete for Victory Points. A card can power movement or change the encounter through its effect. Battles add another decision: which cards should be spent now, and which should be kept?

The original C++ / SFML project established this hybrid. In Next, I redesigned the rules and interaction while moving the playable game into Unity.

OWNERSHIP

My responsibility

I designed the gameplay rules, the interaction flow and the HTML-to-UGUI workflow. AI assisted with implementation of the opponent behavior, prototypes and UI generation. I used simulation results and repeated play to revise the rules, AI behavior and game pacing.

This case focuses on those decisions and the system I used to evaluate them.

ORIGINAL DESIGN AND DEVELOPMENT

From card rules to a playable C++ prototype.

The original Air Command was a collaborative C++ / SFML project. I designed the card-battle system and implemented aircraft movement and card-based combat. Another designer developed the board-related card effects and Victory Point rules; we integrated the two parts and played together to adjust the cards. These records show my design and programming contribution before the later AI-assisted Unity extension.

Define the cards before presenting the battle.

The sheet records cards as rule definitions: a named effect and a point value. Designing these together connects what a card lets the player do with its value in an encounter. This is the original prototype configuration; the Next version uses revised rules and balance.

Make the encounter readable.

The concept separates the two sides and places their point totals beside the card rows. In this example, the defender has six points against the attacker’s five, while an Ambush message reports a two-point bonus. The design makes both the current comparison and the effect that changed it visible.

Implement the rules in C++.

I implemented the movement and card-battle features in C++ and SFML, then integrated them with the collaborative design. Next continues that work with a shared C# rules core, simulations and a new interaction workflow.

SYSTEMS

A rules core that runs in two places.

The Unity presentation and the command-line simulator use the same C# rules source. The simulator project links that source directly, so experiments do not rely on a separately reimplemented game.

Game AI

Turn decisions and battle-choice evaluation.

Headless simulation

Batch games, outcome records and metrics.

Battle decisions use a complete-information, depth-one minimax evaluation of the battle outcome. I adjusted the driving behavior when the simulation revealed patterns that poorly represented the experience I wanted.

EVIDENCE

Measure the pacing, then change the rules.

An early batch rarely reached the intended Victory Point ending and produced too few battles. I revised scoring, card effects and AI behavior across several versions, then examined the next batch.

The later record averages 24.91 full turns and 6.62 battles per game, with 38.31% ending at the turn cap. These are outcomes of several rule, configuration and behavior changes, rather than a controlled test of one variable.

Metrics recalculated from saved CSV records. Simulated outcomes describe these AI policies; they do not establish human enjoyment or fairness.

DESIGN DECISION

Treat the simulator as a design instrument.

Separate a rule problem from a policy problem.

What I noticed

The early saved batch produced few battles, while analysis showed that the AI often kept only one aircraft in play.

What I did

I revised rules, scoring and the driving behavior, then examined another batch. I also considered how complete information weakened information-gathering cards.

What I learned

The final record shows more battles and fewer games ending at the turn cap. Several settings changed together, so I use the data to guide the next question and return to play when judging the experience.

REFLECTION

The experiment also tests the opponent.

Early analysis found that the AI often kept only one aircraft in play. Complete information also reduced the value of information-gathering cards. Those findings changed how I interpreted card-use rates and battle frequency.

I treated the AI policy as part of the experiment, and returned to actual play when judging interaction and pacing.

INTERACTION

A three-step interaction goal.

My interaction goal is to let players complete key actions in three user operations. A typical flow is to select a card, choose an action or target, then confirm or receive feedback. I used this goal to review movement, takeoff, effects and battle choices.

01

Select

Choose a card and inspect its effective points or effect.

02

Choose

Pick an available action or valid target.

03

Resolve

Confirm when needed, then read the result.

I designed a workflow from an AI-assisted HTML prototype to native UGUI. Layout measurement and structured data feed the Unity import; interactive text and controls remain native elements, while decorative layers can be baked separately.

Repeated play shaped the final information hierarchy and feedback. The three-step rule is a design goal; unusual branches still need individual review.

AI-ASSISTED DEVELOPMENT WORKFLOW

From interaction design to native Unity UI.

I designed the HTML-to-UGUI workflow to preserve a fast visual review loop while keeping gameplay in the C# core. AI helped generate and iterate the prototype and importer; I defined the interaction and reviewed the imported result.

01

Describe the interaction

Use the three-operation goal and explicit states to define card selection, targets and feedback.

02

Review the HTML

Iterate layout and interactions in a visual prototype. Its sample behavior is a UI review aid.

03

Export and import

Measure layout into JSON. Bake decorative layers, retain native text and buttons, then assemble shared card prefabs.

04

Verify in the game

Connect the UI to real C# state and play through choices, consumption, replenishment and resolution.

Two card choices must refer to two real instances.

What I noticed

A battle needs an effect card and a point card, but the same instance must not fill both roles. Replenishing a slot must not cause the replacement card to be consumed.

What I did

The documented flow locks the chosen instances and slots before consumption and replenishment. Shared card widgets display the same configured data across views.

What I learned

The project’s retained acceptance notes check the actual Unity buttons and card state. Visual agreement with the HTML is only one part of verifying the interaction.

Process evidence: retained HTML prototypes, layout JSON, style maps, shared UGUI prefabs and Unity acceptance notes. These describe the recorded workflow; this portfolio update does not rerun the Unity checks.
